READY FOR THE ENGINEERS
Episode 1: Our mission
Briefing for the podcast about our CanSat 2027 project

This podcast prepares the students of our CanSat team for the questions of
the engineers in the jury. This episode explains the whole mission: what we
want to find out, how our CanSat works, the forces on the can, the parachute
and the energy, and how we test everything.

Important: our CanSat has not flown yet. All flight numbers in this text come
from a PRACTICE FLIGHT: a full flight that we calculated with physics on the
computer. After the launch we will replace them with the real numbers.

1. WHAT IS A CANSAT?

- A CanSat is a very small satellite, as big as a soft-drink can. Our can is
  66 mm wide and 115 mm high.
- A rocket takes it up to about 1 kilometre. There it leaves the rocket and
  comes down under a parachute.
- During the flight it measures, and it sends its data to the ground by radio,
  every second.
- It works like a real satellite in miniature: a computer, sensors, a radio,
  power, and a ground station.
- Two rules from ESA (the European Space Agency) that we use:
  - the CanSat must survive an acceleration of 20 g;
  - under the parachute it should come down at 8 to 11 metres per second.
- Our CanSat weighs 330 grams, with the parachute.


2. OUR MISSION

The main mission (the same for every team):
- Measure the air temperature and the air pressure.
- Send the data to the ground station at least once per second.
- From the pressure we calculate the height.

Our own mission, our research question:
"What forces and vibrations does a CanSat feel during its flight, and which
zones of the can carry the most load?"

Why is this useful?
- Every real satellite must survive its launch. Before a launch, engineers
  test satellites on a vibration table (a shaker).
- We measure what our CanSat really feels during a real launch.
- Then we calculate where the can is loaded the most. That shows where the can
  must be strong, and where we could save mass.

How the two missions work together:
- To calculate the parachute's drag coefficient (Cd), we need the air density.
- We calculate the air density from our OWN pressure and temperature.
- So the main mission helps our own research.


3. HOW OUR CANSAT WORKS, FROM SENSOR TO SCREEN

The flight computer is a Teensy 4.1: a small and fast microcontroller.

The sensors:
- BMP390: air pressure and temperature. From the pressure: the height.
  Once per second.
- LSM6DSO: acceleration, 416 times per second. We use it for the vibrations
  (with an FFT, see below) and for the biggest shock in each second. It
  measures up to 16 g.
- BNO055: how the can turns and tilts (orientation). 100 times per second.
- GPS: the position, and how fast the can drifts with the wind.
- A small microphone: how loud it is.
- An RFM95W radio: LoRa radio on 868 MHz.

What happens every second:
- The Teensy writes one line with 34 values on the SD card. That is
  everything we measure.
- It also sends 19 values by radio to the ground: the most important ones,
  also the vibration results.

What is an FFT? A calculation that finds which frequencies are in a vibration.
For example: "the can shakes 85 times per second". We split the vibrations
into 5 frequency bands, from slow (0 to 10 Hz) to fast (120 to 208 Hz).

The ground station:
- A Raspberry Pi Pico with the same radio receives the packets.
- A laptop saves every line in a file (a CSV file) and shows two live
  dashboards:
  - Screen 1: graphs of height, temperature, pressure, acceleration, the
    vibrations per frequency band, and the sound.
  - Screen 2: the flight path in 3D, a map, how the can turns, and the load
    on each zone of the can, in colours.

Built to keep going:
- If one sensor fails, the flight program keeps running with the others.
- If the USB cable of the receiver comes loose, the program waits and then
  continues in the same file.

After the flight:
- We copy the file from the SD card.
- Our analysis program reads it and calculates the parachute's Cd, the forces
  on the can, the zones, and the energy. It makes six figures and a table.


4. THE FORCES ON THE CAN

What the sensor measures:
- The accelerometer measures how many g the can feels. Standing still on a
  table: 1 g.
- At 10 g, every part pushes or pulls with 10 times its own weight.
- So the force is: F = m x n x g. Here m is the mass, n is the number of g,
  and g is 9.81 m/s2.

Where the force comes into the can:
- At the BOTTOM during the launch (the rocket pushes) and at the landing (the
  ground pushes). The can is pressed together: compression.
- At the TOP when the parachute pulls. The can is stretched: tension.

The rule:
- A slice of the can must carry all the mass on the other side, seen from
  where the force comes in.
- So the force is biggest close to the place where the force comes in.

Results of the practice flight:
- Launch: 11.2 g. That gives 36 newton at the bottom.
- The parachute opens: 4.1 g. That gives 13 newton at the top, at the
  parachute attachment.
- Steady descent under the parachute: 1 g. About 3 newton at the top.
- Landing: at least 16 g. That gives at least 52 newton at the bottom. We say
  "at least", because our sensor cannot measure more than 16 g.
- The ESA rule of 20 g would give 65 newton at the bottom.

What this means:
- The bottom and the parachute attachment carry the most.
- The middle carries less. The least loaded place is at about 92 mm height
  (the can is 115 mm high).
- Where the material is thin, for example around a hole or at a narrow edge,
  the stress is highest. That is where something breaks first.
- On screen 2 the can changes colour: green means a small load, red means
  20 g on the bottom. The colour stays at the highest value. After the flight
  you can see which zones carried the most.

What we say honestly:
- No sensor measures the force inside the can. We calculate it with a model:
  the measured g times the mass. Engineers work the same way.
- We spread the mass evenly over the height. We can add heavy parts, like the
  battery, at their real height.
- We only calculate forces along the can. The BNO055 shows that the sideways
  forces are small: at most 0.15 g in the steady descent.


5. THE PARACHUTE AND THE ENERGY

The parachute:
- In our code the parachute has a diameter of 45 cm. Its area is 0.159 square
  metres.
- Under the parachute the CanSat falls at a steady speed. Then the drag (the
  push of the air) is exactly as big as the weight.
- From that we calculate the drag coefficient:
  Cd = 2 x m x g / (air density x speed x speed x area)
- The speed comes from the barometer: one height every second.
- The air density comes from our own pressure and temperature:
  air density = pressure / (287 x temperature in kelvin).

Two checks:
- Check 1: the Cd must be the same at every height. High up, the air is
  thinner, so the CanSat falls a bit faster. But the Cd stays the same. In the
  practice flight the Cd was 0.50 at every height.
- Check 2: with this Cd we calculate how hard the parachute pulls, every
  second, and we compare it with the sensor.
  - In the steady descent both say 1.0 g.
  - When the parachute opens, the model says 2.8 g and the sensor 4.1 g.
  - Why the difference? The barometer gives only one height per second. So
    our speed is an average over 2 seconds. When the parachute opened, the
    real top speed was higher than that average.

Descent speed:
- In the practice flight: about 8.5 metres per second, and 8.3 near the
  ground.
- That is inside the ESA range of 8 to 11 metres per second.

Energy (practice flight, highest point 955 metres):
- At the highest point the CanSat has height energy: m x g x h, about
  3.1 kilojoule (3100 joule).
- Under the parachute it loses m x g x v every second: about 27 watt. That is
  as much as a small lamp.
- So the parachute turns about 3 kilojoule into heat and swirls in the air.
- Just before the landing only about 12 joule of motion energy is left.


6. HOW DO WE KNOW IT WORKS?

Our way of working, in five steps:
1. Test each part alone.
2. Test everything together on the table.
3. Test outside: a radio test with the breadboard at 100 metres.
4. A drop test.
5. The launch.

What we built, step by step:
- First a test program for all sensors, with the FFT and a CSV file.
- Then two live dashboards, test tools, a manual, and the full flight
  program.
- Then one button that starts the logger and both dashboards.
- Then a livestream setup, so other people can follow the flight.
- Then the radio test: a receiver on a Raspberry Pi Pico, and radio data in
  the logger and the dashboards.
- Then the analysis of the forces and the energy, with a practice flight.
- Next: the radio test outside at 100 metres, then the drop test, then the
  launch.

The practice flight, our big test:
- We calculated a full flight with physics: a launch at 10 g, a highest point
  of about 950 metres, a parachute, and a landing.
- In this flight we KNOW the right answer: the Cd is 0.50.
- Our analysis program found 0.50 (between 0.48 and 0.52). So the method
  works.

Problems we found and solved:
1. Wrong frequencies. The FFT gave frequencies that were 15 percent too low:
   25 Hz became 21 Hz. The reason: sending a radio packet stopped the program
   for about a quarter of a second, so measurements were missing. The fix: the
   radio now sends in the background, and we use the fixed measuring rate.
   Now the frequency is right within 0.1 Hz.
2. A loose cable. When the USB cable of the receiver came loose, the logging
   program stopped. Now it waits and continues in the same file.
3. A wrong average. Screen 1 showed a Cd that was 10 percent too high. It took
   the average over all seconds, also the first seconds after the parachute
   opened. Now it takes the median of the descent.
4. The wrong force. A script from last year calculated the net force. Under
   the parachute the net force is 0 newton, but the can carries 3 newton. Now
   we use what the sensor feels.
5. The GPS. The GPS had trouble finding satellites. We test it outside, with
   a clear view of the sky. The first fix can take a few minutes.

The limits we say honestly:
- The forces are a model, not a direct measurement.
- Our acceleration sensor stops at 16 g.
- We save one line per second. We see how big a shock is, but not how long
  it lasts.
- The temperature sensor is inside the can, so it reacts slowly.
- There is no real flight yet. The numbers come from the practice flight.


7. QUESTIONS FROM THE ENGINEERS, WITH GOOD ANSWERS

Q: What is your own mission, in one sentence?
A: We measure the forces and vibrations our CanSat feels during the flight,
   and we calculate which zones of the can carry the most load.

Q: Why is that useful?
A: Every satellite must survive its launch. We measure what a launch really
   does to our can, and we find where the can must be strong and where we
   could save mass.

Q: You have no force sensor. How do you measure a force?
A: The accelerometer measures how many g the can feels. We multiply by the
   mass: F = m x n x g. That is a model. We say that honestly, and engineers
   work the same way.

Q: Which part of your CanSat carries the most load?
A: The bottom, at the launch and at the landing. And the parachute attachment
   when the parachute opens. In the practice flight: at least 52 newton on the
   bottom at the landing, and 13 newton at the attachment.

Q: Your sensor stops at 16 g, and ESA asks 20 g. Is that a problem?
A: The CanSat must survive 20 g. The sensor does not have to measure it. If a
   shock is bigger than 16 g, we report "at least 16 g". 16 g is the largest
   range of this sensor.

Q: Why only one line per second?
A: ESA asks for at least one per second, and the radio needs time to send.
   Inside the Teensy, the sensors measure much faster: 416 times per second
   for the vibrations. We keep the biggest shock of every second, so we do
   not miss it.

Q: How do you know your Cd is right?
A: Three ways. First, in our practice flight we knew the answer, 0.50, and we
   found 0.50. Second, the Cd must be the same at every height, and it is.
   Third, we compare the parachute model with the sensor.

Q: Why do you need the temperature for the parachute?
A: For the air density: pressure divided by 287 times the temperature in
   kelvin. Warmer air is thinner. So our main mission helps our own research.

Q: What if the radio loses packets?
A: The SD card keeps everything: 34 values every second. The radio is for the
   live view. The analysis after the flight uses the SD card.

Q: What if a sensor fails during the flight?
A: The flight program checks every sensor at the start, and it keeps running
   with the other sensors.

Q: What was your biggest problem?
A: The FFT gave frequencies that were 15 percent too low. The radio stopped
   the program for a quarter of a second, so measurements were missing. Now
   the radio sends in the background, and the frequencies are right within
   0.1 Hz.

Q: Where does the energy go when the CanSat comes down?
A: Into the air. The parachute turns about 3 kilojoule into heat and swirls,
   about 27 watt during the descent. At the landing only about 12 joule of
   motion energy is left.

Q: What will you do after the launch?
A: We copy the file from the SD card and run our analysis program. We compare
   the real flight with the practice flight, and we check the ESA rules: a
   descent speed of 8 to 11 metres per second, and 20 g.

WORDS WE USE

- g: the acceleration of gravity, 9.81 m/s2. "4 g" means four times your own
  weight.
- Accelerometer: a sensor that measures acceleration, in g.
- Load: the force that a part of the can must carry.
- Compression: pressed together. Tension: stretched.
- Model: a calculation that copies the real world, with formulas and measured
  numbers.
- FFT: a calculation that finds the frequencies in a vibration.
- Frequency: how many times per second something shakes, in hertz (Hz).
- Drag: the push of the air against a moving object.
- Drag coefficient (Cd): a number that says how well the parachute catches
  the air.
- Air density: how many kilograms of air there are in one cubic metre.
- Barometer: a sensor that measures the air pressure. We use it for the
  height.
- Telemetry: the data that the CanSat sends to the ground by radio.
- Ground station: the receiver and the laptop on the ground.
- LoRa: a kind of radio that can send small messages over a long distance.
- Practice flight: a full flight that we calculated on the computer, with
  a known answer, to test our analysis.
